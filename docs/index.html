Visual Instruction Tuning Aligns Modalities through Abstraction

Luis Palacios*, Lorenzo Basile*, Diego Doimo†, Alberto Cazzaniga†
Area Science Park, Trieste, Italy
*Equal contribution   †Equal supervision
arXiv Paper (PDF) Code Checkpoints & data BibTeX
TL;DR. Visual instruction tuning does not modify the representation space within the LLM backbone uniformly, but focuses on an intermediate group of semantically-rich layers. We establish this on seven VLM architectures through causal interventions, semantic probing, and geometric similarity analysis, and validate it with localized fine-tuning: updating only the intermediate layers approximately preserves full fine-tuning performance, especially on vision-centric tasks.

Abstract

Visual instruction tuning effectively adapts a pre-trained Large Language Model (LLM) to process image information alongside text. Yet, it remains unclear how visual features are embedded into the layer-wise hierarchy of abstractions of the LLM backbone. Across a diverse set of vision-language architectures, we show that instruction tuning primarily serves as a bridge, embedding visual features directly into the intermediate semantic layers of the LLM, bypassing the early layers devoted to unimodal processing. With probing analyses and causal interventions, we show that these intermediate layers are the semantic core of vision-language processing and play a critical role in the performance on a broad set of multimodal benchmarks. In addition, by comparing the geometry of semantically equivalent visual and textual representations, we find that fine-tuning extends and strengthens the existing abstraction phase, aligning visual features with pre-existing textual ones. Finally, we confirm the functional role of this localized alignment by restricting fine-tuning to intermediate layers alone: this strategy preserves the performance of full fine-tuning on vision-centric benchmarks while reducing training time. Our results suggest that multimodal integration is a localized phenomenon driven by the repurposing of the internal abstraction engine of the LLM.

Contributions

  1. Through interventional analysis, we show that intermediate layers are necessary for vision–language performance: ablating them causes the largest drop across all layer ranges on a diverse set of tasks, identifying them as the main locus of cross-modal communication.
  2. By probing the semantic content of hidden representations, we find that data abstractions emerge in these intermediate layers. Comparing pre-trained and fine-tuned representations of semantically equivalent visual and textual prompts reveals a deep impact of instruction tuning in aligning visual and textual abstractions.
  3. Localized instruction tuning shows that the abstraction layers are also sufficient for multimodal integration: restricting fine-tuning to them closely matches full fine-tuning at lower training cost, with the largest gains on the most vision-centric tasks.

Setup

Models. LLaVA-1.5-7B/13B, LLaVA-OneVision-1.5-4B/8B, InternVL2-8B and Cambrian-8B, each compared in its pre-trained checkpoint and after visual instruction tuning. To verify that the findings hold beyond self-attention, we also analyze Llama-3.2-Vision-90B, a VLM based on cross-attention whose pre-trained LLM weights remain frozen.

Data. A broad range of vision and vision-language benchmarks covering multiple-choice and open-ended question answering, captioning, classification and compositional reasoning, plus text-only benchmarks.

Methods. Causal interventions (attention knockout, which removes image-to-text information flow, and layer skipping of visual tokens); semantic probing (label overlap, linear probes, logit lens); and a comparison of semantically equivalent prompts, where the image is replaced by a caption conveying the same information, using the information imbalance, a nearest-neighbor-based measure of directional predictability between representation spaces.

Key findings

1. Intermediate layers drive vision–language performance

Layer-skipping and attention knockout show that cross-modal integration is mainly localized in intermediate layers. In LLaVA, image processing and cross-modal communication are simultaneous, while in OneVision a range of early layers (6 to 12) performs unimodal processing: performance is unaffected by the cross-modal attention knockout there, but is greatly damaged if the visual embeddings are removed.

2. Semantic abstractions emerge in the intermediate layers

In the early-mid layers, the representations accumulate the semantic properties of the inputs, while the function of the last layers is to understand the correct letter-to-class assignment and align the representation with the unembedding directions for generation.

3. Instruction tuning aligns visual and textual abstractions

Visual instruction tuning substantially increases the alignment between semantically equivalent visual and textual representations. This effect is concentrated in the intermediate abstraction layers, while later layers preserve a residual asymmetry in which textual representations remain more informative than visual ones (more manifestly for OneVision).

4. Fine-tuning only the abstraction layers matches full fine-tuning

Fine-tuning only the abstraction region (layers 6–16 in LLaVA-7B, 8–25 in OneVision-4B) reaches 98.8% and 100.4% of full fine-tuning, while reducing training time by 14% and 24%. The complementary windows, which have an equal or larger number of layers and additionally train the embedding and unembedding parameters, lag behind by more than 4% and 2%, respectively. As noted in the paper's limitations, the objective was mechanistic characterization rather than performance maximization, so this configuration may not represent the global optimum.

5. Localized fine-tuning helps most when the image matters
